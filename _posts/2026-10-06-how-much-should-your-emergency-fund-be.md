**The short answer:** a common guideline is **3 to 6 months of essential expenses**. If your income is unpredictable, you're the only earner, or you'd take a long time to find a new job, aim for the higher end or more. If you're paying off high-interest debt, a smaller starter fund (often $500 to $1,000) is a reasonable first step.

## Base it on expenses, not income

An emergency fund has to cover what you **must** pay if your income stops, not your whole lifestyle. Add up your essential monthly costs:

- Housing (rent or mortgage)
- Utilities
- Groceries
- Insurance (health, car, home or renters)
- Transport to work or job interviews
- Minimum debt payments
- Childcare and essential medical costs

Leave out things you'd cut in an emergency: eating out, streaming, holidays, extra debt payments.

### Example

| Essential expense | Monthly |
|---|---|
| Rent | $1,400 |
| Utilities | $180 |
| Groceries | $450 |
| Insurance | $220 |
| Transport | $160 |
| Phone | $60 |
| Debt minimums | $105 |
| **Total** | **$2,575** |

- 3 months: **$7,725**
- 6 months: **$15,450**

## Should you aim for 3 months or 6?

Lean toward **3 months** if:

- You have two stable incomes in the household.
- Your job is in high demand and you could find another quickly.
- You have no dependents.

Lean toward **6 months or more** if:

- You're self-employed or your income varies a lot.
- You're the only earner, or you have dependents.
- You work in an industry with seasonal or uncertain hiring.
- You own a home or an older car (more things can break).

## What counts as an emergency?

A useful test: it's **unexpected, necessary and urgent**. Job loss, a medical bill, a car repair you need for work, an emergency flight home. A sale, a holiday or a planned purchase isn't, and those belong in [sinking funds](/sinking-funds-explained/) instead.

## Where to keep it

Somewhere **safe, separate and quick to reach**:

- A high-yield savings account at an FDIC-insured bank (or NCUA-insured credit union) is the most common choice. Deposits are insured up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category.
- Keep it in a **different account** from your everyday checking so you don't spend it by accident.
- Avoid investing it in stocks. The point is that it's there, at full value, on the worst day.

## How to build it when money is tight

1. **Start with a small target.** $500 covers many common surprises. Reaching it quickly builds the habit.
2. **Automate it.** Even $25 a week the day after payday adds up to $1,300 a year.
3. **Save windfalls.** Tax refunds, bonuses and gifts can jump-start the fund.
4. **Refill after you use it.** Using it is the whole point. Pause other goals until it's back.

## Emergency fund or debt first?

Most people do both in stages: build a starter fund, then focus on high-interest debt (see [snowball vs avalanche](/debt-snowball-vs-avalanche/)), then grow the fund to the full 3 to 6 months. Without any buffer, the next surprise tends to go straight onto a credit card.

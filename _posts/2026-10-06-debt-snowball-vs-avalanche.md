**The short answer:** the debt avalanche (highest interest rate first) almost always costs less in interest. The debt snowball (smallest balance first) gives you quicker wins, which helps many people stick with it. If the interest rates on your debts are close together, the difference is small, so pick the one you'll actually follow.

Below is a full example so you can see the real difference, not just the theory.

## How both methods work

Both methods use the same three rules:

1. **Pay the minimum on every debt, every month.** Missing a minimum costs you fees and hurts your credit.
2. **Put every extra dollar on one "target" debt.**
3. **When the target is paid off, roll its whole payment into the next target.** This is the "snowball" effect, and both methods use it.

The only difference is the **order** of the targets:

| Method | Target order | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Snowball | Smallest balance first | Fast early wins, fewer bills sooner |
| Avalanche | Highest interest rate (APR) first | Least interest paid overall |

## The worked example

Say you have four debts and can pay **$200 a month on top of your minimums**:

| Debt | Balance | APR | Minimum |
|---|---|---|---|
| Store card | $600 | 27% | $25 |
| Credit card | $4,200 | 22% | $105 |
| Personal loan | $2,500 | 12% | $85 |
| Car loan | $8,500 | 7% | $260 |

Total minimums are $475, so the total monthly payment is $675. We simulated both methods month by month, charging interest monthly at APR ÷ 12.

**Snowball order:** store card → personal loan → credit card → car loan
**Avalanche order:** store card → credit card → personal loan → car loan

(The store card has both the smallest balance and the highest rate, so it goes first either way.)

### Results

| | Snowball | Avalanche |
|---|---|---|
| Store card paid off | Month 3 | Month 3 |
| Second debt paid off | Personal loan, month 11 | Credit card, month 18 |
| Third debt paid off | Credit card, month 21 | Personal loan, month 21 |
| Debt-free | Month 27 | Month 27 |
| Total interest | about $2,253 | about $2,055 |

**Avalanche saved about $198 in interest.** Both finished in the same month here, because the car loan (the biggest, lowest-rate debt) is last either way and sets the finish line.

But look at the second row. With snowball, you'd have **two debts completely gone by month 11**. With avalanche, you'd wait until month 18 for your second win. For some people, those seven months are where motivation runs out.

### What the extra $200 is worth

For comparison, paying only the minimums (and still rolling payments forward as debts close) would take **43 months** and cost about **$4,262 in interest**. The extra $200 a month cuts 16 months and roughly $2,000 in interest, whichever method you choose. The order matters far less than the extra payment.

## Which should you choose?

Choose **avalanche** if:

- Your interest rates are far apart (for example a 25% card and a 5% loan).
- You're motivated by the total number, not by closing accounts.
- Your highest-rate debt isn't enormous, so your first win still comes reasonably soon.

Choose **snowball** if:

- You've tried to pay off debt before and lost steam.
- You have several small balances you could clear within a few months.
- The rates are fairly similar, so the interest cost of snowball is small.

A practical middle ground: **knock out any tiny balances first for the quick wins, then switch to avalanche for the rest.** Nothing says you have to pick a pure method.

## Common mistakes

- **Stopping the rollover.** When a debt is paid off, it's tempting to spend the freed-up payment. Keep your total monthly payment the same until everything is gone.
- **No emergency buffer.** Without even a small cash cushion, a car repair goes straight back on a card. Many people keep $500 to $1,000 aside before attacking debt hard. See [how much to keep in an emergency fund](/how-much-should-your-emergency-fund-be/).
- **Ignoring promotional rates.** A 0% balance transfer that expires in six months will jump to a high rate. Treat it as a high-rate debt for ordering purposes once the promo ends.

## How to run your own numbers

You need four things for each debt: the balance, the APR, the minimum payment, and the extra amount you can pay each month. Then:

1. List the debts in your chosen order.
2. Each month, add interest (balance × APR ÷ 12) to each debt.
3. Pay every minimum, then put the extra on the first debt that still has a balance.
4. When a debt hits zero, add its minimum to the extra.

Doing this by hand for 20 to 40 months is tedious, which is why most people use a spreadsheet or a calculator. If you'd rather pay off credit cards specifically, see our guide on [how to pay off credit card debt faster](/how-to-pay-off-credit-card-debt-faster/).

**The short answer:** build your budget around your **lowest typical month**, not your average. Rank your spending so you know exactly what gets paid first in a lean month. Then, once you can, send all income into a separate holding account and pay yourself the **same "salary" every month** from it. The holding account absorbs the swings so your budget doesn't have to.

This works for freelancers, gig workers, commission sales, tipped jobs, seasonal work and small-business owners alike.

## Why normal budgeting breaks with uneven pay

A standard [monthly budget](/how-to-make-a-monthly-budget/) assumes the same paycheck arrives every month. With irregular income, two things go wrong:

- **Budgeting from your average** feels reasonable, but roughly half your months come in below average. Those months end on a credit card.
- **Spending what you earn** feels fine in a good month, but there's nothing left over when the slow month arrives.

The fix is to separate *when money arrives* from *when you spend it*.

## Step 1: Look at the last 12 months of income

Pull your bank statements or invoices and write down what you actually took home each month (after any taxes withheld or set aside). Here's an example for a freelancer:

| Month | Income | Month | Income |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jan | $3,100 | Jul | $3,400 |
| Feb | $4,800 | Aug | $4,600 |
| Mar | $2,600 | Sep | $2,700 |
| Apr | $3,900 | Oct | $3,800 |
| May | $5,200 | Nov | $5,500 |
| Jun | $2,900 | Dec | $3,300 |

Over the year that's **$45,800**, an average of about **$3,817** a month. But the months range from **$2,600** to **$5,500**. Budgeting $3,800 a month would have left this person short in six of the twelve months.

If you have less than a year of history, use what you have and be conservative.

## Step 2: Find your baseline

Your **baseline** is the lowest amount you can reasonably count on in a month. In the example, that's **$2,600**. (If one month was a genuine one-off, like an illness or a gap between contracts, you can leave it out, but be honest with yourself.)

Your baseline budget must cover your essentials. If it can't, that's important to know now rather than in the middle of a slow month.

## Step 3: Rank your spending into tiers

Instead of one flat list, sort every expense into tiers. In a lean month you pay tier 1 first, then tier 2, and so on until the money runs out.

1. **Tier 1, survival:** housing, utilities, groceries, insurance, transport to work, minimum debt payments.
2. **Tier 2, important:** [sinking funds](/sinking-funds-explained/) for irregular bills, phone, childcare, savings toward your [emergency fund](/how-much-should-your-emergency-fund-be/).
3. **Tier 3, goals:** extra debt payments, retirement contributions, [savings goals](/how-much-to-save-each-month-for-a-goal/).
4. **Tier 4, extras:** eating out, entertainment, upgrades.

The aim is for tier 1 to fit inside your baseline. Everything above the baseline funds tiers 2 to 4 in order.

### What the extra money does

In the example, income above the $2,600 baseline adds up to **$14,600** over the year. That's the money that funds tiers 2 to 4. In March there's nothing extra; in November there's $2,900. Ranking your spending in advance means a good month goes to the right places instead of disappearing.

## Step 4: Pay yourself a salary from a holding account

This is the step that makes irregular income feel regular.

1. Open a separate savings account and call it something like "Income holding."
2. Send **every** payment you receive into it.
3. Once a month, on the same day, transfer a fixed "salary" into your everyday checking account.
4. Budget your checking account exactly like someone with a steady paycheck.

How big should the salary be? A sensible starting point is a little **below** your 12-month average. In the example, $3,600 a month (about $217 under the average).

### Why you need a starting buffer

Here's what happens to the holding account if you start paying yourself $3,600 a month **with nothing in it**:

| | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Balance after salary | −$500 | $700 | −$300 | $0 | $1,600 | $900 |

It goes negative twice in the first three months. Now the same thing with a **$2,000 starting buffer**:

| | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Balance after salary | $1,500 | $2,700 | $1,700 | $2,000 | $3,600 | $2,900 |

The lowest point all year is $1,500, and the account ends December at **$4,600**. (We ran these month by month from the income table above.)

If you don't have a buffer yet, don't wait. Start with Step 3: pay tier 1 from every payment, and push every dollar above your baseline into the holding account until it has one or two months of salary in it. Then switch to the fixed salary.

## Step 5: Review every quarter

Every three months, look at the holding account:

- **Growing steadily?** You can raise your salary a little, or move the surplus to goals.
- **Shrinking?** Lower your salary, or trim tier 4, before it becomes a problem.
- **Big surplus after a strong season?** Top up your emergency fund first, then goals.

## If you're self-employed: set money aside for taxes

If no tax is withheld from your income, keep that money separate the moment you're paid, before it reaches the holding account. Many freelancers use a third account just for taxes. How much to set aside depends on your income, deductions and location, so check with a tax professional or the IRS guidance on [estimated taxes](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/estimated-taxes).

## Common mistakes

- **Treating a great month as the new normal.** One strong month doesn't change your baseline.
- **Skipping sinking funds.** Irregular bills hit hardest when income is also irregular.
- **Mixing business and personal money.** Separate accounts make every step above easier.
- **Waiting for the perfect buffer.** Even half a month's salary in the holding account takes a lot of stress out of slow months.

## Quick checklist

- Write down 12 months of take-home income.
- Find your baseline (lowest typical month).
- Sort expenses into four tiers; make tier 1 fit the baseline.
- Open a holding account and send all income there.
- Build a buffer, then pay yourself a fixed monthly salary a little below your average.
- Review the salary every quarter.

Prefer a fixed-percentage approach to start with? The [50/30/20 rule](/50-30-20-rule-explained/) works on top of this system too: just apply it to your salary instead of to each payment.

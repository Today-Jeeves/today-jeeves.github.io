**The short answer:** stop making the minimum payment, pick a fixed monthly amount you can afford, aim it at one card at a time, and lower your interest rate where you can. The fixed payment is the biggest lever by far.

## Why minimum payments keep you stuck

Card minimums are designed to shrink as your balance shrinks, which stretches repayment out for years. Many issuers set the minimum at about 1% of the balance plus that month's interest, with a floor of around $25.

Here's what that means for a **$5,000 balance at 22% APR** (our calculation, assuming no new purchases):

| Monthly payment | Time to pay off | Total interest |
|---|---|---|
| Minimum only (1% + interest, $25 floor) | about 19 years | about $8,100 |
| Fixed $150 | 52 months (4.3 years) | about $2,800 |
| Fixed $250 | 26 months (2.2 years) | about $1,286 |
| Fixed $400 | 15 months | about $732 |

On the minimum, you'd pay back more than double what you borrowed. Your own statement shows a similar warning box, required by law in the US, with your actual numbers.

## 1. Stop adding to the balance

Paying down a card you're still using is like bailing out a boat with a hole in it. Take the card out of your wallet and remove it from online stores and phone wallets. You don't need to close the account (closing it can lower your available credit and affect your score), just stop using it.

## 2. Choose a fixed payment, not "whatever's left"

Look at your budget and pick a number you can pay every month, even in a tight month. Set it as an automatic payment so it happens before you can spend the money. If you don't have a budget yet, start with [how to make a monthly budget](/how-to-make-a-monthly-budget/).

Once the payment is fixed, keep it fixed. As the balance falls, more of each payment goes to principal and less to interest, so the debt disappears faster every month.

## 3. If you have several cards, pick an order

Pay the minimum on every card, then put everything extra on one target card:

- **Avalanche:** highest APR first. Lowest total interest.
- **Snowball:** smallest balance first. Quicker wins.

When the target card is paid off, roll its whole payment onto the next card. We ran both on the same debts in [debt snowball vs avalanche](/debt-snowball-vs-avalanche/).

## 4. Ask for a lower interest rate

It costs nothing to call the number on the back of your card and ask. Say how long you've been a customer and that you're working to pay the balance down. It doesn't always work, but even a few points off your APR saves real money on a large balance.

## 5. Consider a balance transfer, carefully

A 0% intro balance transfer card can pause interest for a set period, often 12 to 21 months. It can work well **if**:

- You qualify (they usually need good credit).
- The transfer fee, typically 3% to 5% of the amount moved, is less than the interest you'd save.
- You can pay off the balance, or most of it, before the promo ends.
- You don't run the old card back up.

Divide the transferred balance by the number of promo months to get the payment that clears it in time. If you can't afford that, the remaining balance will start charging the regular rate, which is often high.

## 6. Find extra money for a few months

A short burst of extra payments goes a long way because it cuts the balance that interest is charged on. Ideas that don't require a new job:

- Cancel subscriptions you haven't used in the past month.
- Sell things you no longer use.
- Put any tax refund, bonus or overtime straight onto the target card.
- Pause one "want" category in your budget for 90 days.

## 7. Keep a small emergency buffer

If a car repair or a vet bill goes straight back on the card, you lose ground. Many people keep a starter emergency fund of $500 to $1,000 while paying off debt, then build it up after. See [how much to keep in an emergency fund](/how-much-should-your-emergency-fund-be/).

## When to get help

If you can't cover the minimums, or debt is growing despite your efforts, a nonprofit credit counseling agency can review your situation and may offer a debt management plan with lower rates. The CFPB has guidance on [choosing a credit counselor](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-credit-counseling-en-1451/). Be wary of companies that charge large upfront fees or promise to make debt disappear.

## A quick plan you can start today

1. Write down every card: balance, APR, minimum.
2. Pick your fixed total monthly payment.
3. Choose snowball or avalanche and list the cards in that order.
4. Set up autopay for every minimum, plus a payment for the extra on your target card.
5. Each time a card hits zero, move its payment to the next one.
